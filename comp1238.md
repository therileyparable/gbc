## Week 1 - History
- Johannes Gutenberg (Germany, ~1450s): Often credited with inventing printing, but more accurately, he perfected and popularized the movable type printing press in Europe. His success was significantly driven by business and economic factors.
- Serif vs. Sans Serif: Serif fonts have small decorative strokes (serifs) at the end of letter strokes (like Times New Roman). Sans serif fonts ("sans" means "without" in French) do not (like Arial).
- Early Literature: Epic of Gilgamesh (~4000 years ago, 2000 BC) - one of the earliest known long-form literary works. Story of a king's quest for immortality after his friend's death, includes a flood myth prototype. Shows the human desire to achieve immortality through writing stories.

 ## Week 2 - Bits and Bytes
 - Binary System Basics:
   1. Uses only two digits: 0 and 1
   2. Least Significant Bit (LSB): The rightmost bit (represents 2^0 = 1).
   3. Most Significant Bit (MSB): The leftmost bit (represents the highest power of 2).
   4. Conversion (Binary to Decimal): Assign powers of 2 to each bit position, starting from 2^0 on the right (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128 for an 8-bit byte); Sum the powers of 2 where the bit is 1.
- Ascii (American Standard Code for Information Interchange): A standard mapping of byte values (0-127) to characters.
- The core idea is that everything in a computer is ultimately stored as bits, and humans/software impose meaning and structure on those bits.

## Week 3 - Text Structure
- Indentation: Whitespace at the beginning of a line or block of text.
  1. Used to visually group related lines and show hierarchical structure (e.g., in code, data formats like JSON).
  2. Makes text much easier for humans to parse visually, even if the computer ignores it.
     1. Common examples and names (useful to know for searching/discussing):
        1. \. - Period, full stop, decimal point.
        2. \- - Hyphen, minus sign. (Note: Publishing has many dash types, ASCII usually only one short one).
        3. \() - Parentheses, round brackets.
        4. \[] - Square brackets.
        5. \{} - Curly braces, curly brackets.
        6. \<> - Angled brackets, less than/greater than signs. (Dual meaning: math comparison vs. HTML tags).
        7. \+ - Plus sign.
        8. \* - Star, asterisk.
        9. / - Slash, forward slash, division symbol.
        10. % - Percent sign.
        11. \# - Hash mark, pound sign, number sign.
        12. $ - Dollar sign.
        13. & - Ampersand, 'and' sign.
        14. @ - At sign.
        15. \\ - Backslash.
        16. \_ - Underscore, low dash. (Often treated like a letter in programming variable names).
        17. ^ - Caret, hat. (Often used for exponentiation).
        18. ~ - Tilde, tilda. (Used for approximation, or in some languages over letters like 'ñ').
        19. \` - Back tick, back quote.
        20. \| - Vertical line, pipe. (Named after the Unix/Linux pipe operation).

## Week 4 - Keyboards and Editors
- Keyboards and mice are our primary interface to convey thoughts/intentions to the computer. Mastering the keyboard is key.
  1. Layouts; Distinguish between:
     1. Physical Layout: Key arrangement and shape (e.g. QWERTY—most common globally or regional variants—such as the German QWERTZ setup that swaps some keys (Z/Y) and has different punctuation placement or the French AZWERTY setup which simply swaps the punctuation)
     2. Visual Labeling: What's printed on the keys (e.g. multilangual keyboard setups that have both English and one other language printed on the keycaps)
     3. Functional/Software Layout: What characters are produced (can be changed in OS settings, e.g., switching languages)
   2. Modifier Keys: Keys like Ctrl, Shift, Alt, Windows/Command. They have no function alone but modify other keys (Windows+Tab -> Swap between currently opened applications, Ctrl+C -> Copy)
 - Text Editors
   1. Distinctions:
      1. Word Processors (e.g., Microsoft Word): Designed for rich text formatting (fonts, colors, layout). Convenient for documents intended for human reading/printing. Very inconvenient for writing code (auto-correct, hidden formatting).
      2. Text Editors (e.g., Notepad, Notepad++, VS Code, Vim): Designed for plain text. Each byte typically corresponds directly to a character via ASCII. Provide precise control over the raw text file. Essential for writing code.
   2. IDEs (Integrated Development Environments):
      1. Combine a text editor with many other tools for software development (compilers, debuggers, build tools, version control integration, etc.).
      2. Often optimized for specific programming languages (e.g., WebStorm for web dev, PyCharm for Python, Visual Studio for C#/Microsoft stack).
- Regex (Regular Expressions):
  1. Concept: A powerful, complex language for describing text patterns. Can look like "line noise"
  2. Literal characters match themselves.
Character Sets: [abc] (matches 'a', 'b', or 'c'), [A-Z] (matches any uppercase letter), [0-9] or \d (matches any digit).
Quantifiers: + (one or more of the preceding element), * (zero or more), {n,m} (between n and m times).
- Practicality of Regex: Useful in editors for advanced search/replace. ChatGPT is very good at generating regex patterns, then you can test them in a debugger like RegExr. Debugging complex regex is notoriously difficult.
